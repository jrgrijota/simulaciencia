# Teacher guide: balancing equations simulation

## Quick reference

The simulation represents chemical reactions with atoms and molecules: it lets you balance equations while watching the atoms rearrange, check Lavoisier's and Proust's laws with a balance, and find the limiting reactant and the yield. Its main use is in ESO 3 (Year 10 in the UK, 9th grade in the US), with applications in ESO 4 and Bachillerato 1.

## How the simulation works

The simulation has three modes, chosen in the “Mode” card: “Balancing (Lavoisier)”, “Definite proportions (Proust)” and “Limiting reactant and yield”. In all three, each ball is an atom with its symbol and colour, and each group of joined balls is a molecule or the formula unit of an ionic compound. The “Key” card shows the elements in the chosen reaction with their atomic mass.

| Mode | What the teacher controls | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Balancing (Lavoisier) | “Reaction” (16 equations in three groups: “Simple”, “Combustion” and “Harder”). The coefficient of each substance, from 1 to 9, with the − and + buttons. “Live count” switch and “✓ Check”, “▶ Show the reaction” and “↺ All to 1” buttons | The equation with its coefficients, the particles of the reactants and products, the “ATOM COUNT” for each element with ✓ or ✗ and a “BALANCE (TOTAL MASS)” with the mass on each side |
| Definite proportions (Proust) | “Reaction”: seven compounds (H₂O, H₂O₂, CO₂, CO, NH₃, FeS and MgO). Starting amount of each reactant. “▶ React”, “↺ Restart” and “🗑 Clear the results table” buttons | The container with the particles, the mass ratio of the two elements in the compound, its percentage composition, a graph of combined masses and a table with each experiment |
| Limiting reactant and yield | “Reaction” (seven balanced reactions). Starting amount of each reactant, “Yield” from 10 % to 100 % and “Your prediction: which reactant runs out?” | The container with the particles, a “Start / Change / End” table with the final mass of each substance, bars for the amounts and the results: limiting reactant, reactant in excess, theoretical product, actual product and yield |

In all three modes, the “Each particle represents” drop-down changes the scale. With “One molecule or atom (mass in u)” the amounts are particles and the masses are atomic mass units. With “One mole (mass in g)” each particle drawn is one mole and the masses are given in grams: the numbers do not change, only the unit.

Four elements support the explanation:

- **The flying atoms.** In balancing mode, “▶ Show the reaction” takes each atom of the reactants to a slot in the products. If the equation is not balanced, some atoms stay in the reactants surrounded by a red ring (“Atoms left over”) or dashed empty slots remain in the products (“atoms missing”). In the reactor, each group of reactants breaks apart and its atoms form the products.
- **The atom count.** One row per element with the atoms in the reactants and in the products. With “Live count” switched off, the table and the balance show “?” until “✓ Check” is pressed.
- **The balance and the mass bar.** The balance in balancing mode tips towards the side with more mass and levels out when the equation is balanced. In the reactor, the “TOTAL MASS IN THE CONTAINER” bar shares out the mass between the substances, and its total length does not change while the reaction takes place.
- **The status message.** Below the equation appears “✗ Not balanced…”, “✓ Balanced equation: atoms and mass are conserved” or, if the coefficients have a common factor, “✓ Balanced, but it can be simplified”.

In Proust mode and limiting reactant mode, the amount sliders move in steps of the coefficient: with 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O, hydrogen goes up 2 at a time. That way the limiting reactant always runs out completely. The “How does it work?” drop-down, closed at the start, summarises the three modes.

## Use in each year group

In ESO 3 the simulation supports balancing simple equations and Lavoisier's and Proust's laws as evidence for the atomic-molecular model. In ESO 4 it is used to balance combustion reactions and for the first stoichiometric calculations with moles, including yield. In Bachillerato 1 it supports the laws of chemical combination (Dalton's law too), percentage composition, the limiting reactant and yield. The curriculum content quoted is from the LOMLOE curriculum of the Community of Madrid (Spain), in our own translation.

The masses quoted are those the simulation shows, using IUPAC atomic masses rounded to two decimal places.

### ESO 2: occasional use

*Ages 13–14 · Year 9 in the UK · 8th grade in the US*

There is no content on balancing. Balancing mode can illustrate “Physical and chemical changes in material systems” and “Introduction to chemical reactions” (block C), and “Atoms and molecules: common, well-known elements and compounds” (block B). With “Formation of water” already balanced (2, 1 and 2), “▶ Show the reaction” shows that the hydrogen and oxygen atoms separate and join up again to form a new substance, without appearing or disappearing.

### ESO 3: balancing and Lavoisier's and Proust's laws

*Ages 14–15 · Year 10 in the UK · 9th grade in the US*

**Curriculum content.** Block C: “Microscopic interpretation of chemical reactions” with “Balancing simple chemical equations”, and “Applying the law of conservation of mass (Lavoisier's law) and the law of definite proportions (Proust's law): applying these laws as experimental evidence that validates the atomic-molecular model of matter. Simple stoichiometric calculations”. Block B: “Atomic mass and molecular mass”.

**Objectives.** Balance simple equations by changing only the coefficients. Explain conservation of mass through conservation of atoms. Check that a compound always has the same mass ratio of its elements.

**Teaching sequence (15–20 min).**

1. Balancing mode, “Formation of water” with every coefficient at 1. The count shows H 2 against 2 ✓ and O 2 against 1 ✗; the balance tips: 34.01 u against 18.02 u. Press “▶ Show the reaction”: “✗ Atoms left over: 1 O”.
2. Ask how to fix it. If someone suggests writing H₂O₂, explain that this is hydrogen peroxide, a different substance: the simulation only lets you change coefficients. Put a 2 in front of H₂O: now oxygen balances, but hydrogen does not (2 against 4). “▶ Show the reaction”: “✗ Atoms missing: 2 H”.
3. Put a 2 in front of H₂. “✓ Balanced equation: atoms and mass are conserved” appears and the balance levels out: 36.03 u on each side. Show the reaction: “✓ Every atom finds its place”. State Lavoisier's law.
4. Count the particles in each box: 3 molecules in the reactants and 2 in the products. The statistics, on the other hand, give 6 atoms on each side. Atoms and mass are conserved, not molecules.
5. Switch off “Live count” and ask students to balance “Synthesis of ammonia” (1, 3, 2) and “Oxidation of magnesium” (2, 1, 2) before pressing “✓ Check”.
6. Proust mode, “Water (H₂O)”, with the starting values (4 H₂ and 4 O₂). “▶ React”: 4 H₂O form and 2 O₂ are left over. The table records m(O)/m(H) = 7.94. Raise H₂ to 24 and repeat: 8 H₂O form, 16 H₂ are left over and the ratio is still 7.94. The points on the graph fall on the same straight line: this is Proust's law.

### ESO 4: balancing combustion reactions and stoichiometry

*Ages 15–16 · Year 11 in the UK · 10th grade in the US*

**Curriculum content.** Block C: “Chemical equations: balancing chemical equations and making qualitative and quantitative predictions based on stoichiometry”, with “Balancing chemical equations. Stoichiometric calculations. Yield of a reaction”, and “Qualitative description of chemical reactions of interest: combustion reactions”. Block B: “Atomic and molecular mass. The concept of the mole. The Avogadro constant”.

**Objectives.** Balance combustion reactions of hydrocarbons. Interpret the coefficients as a ratio in moles. Calculate the amount of product from the amount of a reactant and apply the yield.

**Teaching sequence (15–20 min).**

1. Balancing mode, “Combustion of methane”. Balance in the usual order: first carbon (already balanced), then hydrogen (2 in front of H₂O) and finally oxygen (2 in front of O₂). The balance shows 80.04 u on each side.
2. “Combustion of propane” with “Live count” switched off: students suggest the coefficients (1, 5, 3 and 4) and they are checked with “✓ Check”. Mass on each side: 204.09 u.
3. “Combustion of ethane”: balancing the oxygen gives 7/2 in front of O₂; multiply everything by 2 to get 2, 7, 4 and 6. The other way round, if someone balances water with 4, 2 and 4, the simulation warns: “✓ Balanced, but it can be simplified: divide the coefficients by 2”.
4. Change “Each particle represents” to “One mole (mass in g)”. The balance switches to grams with the same numbers: the molar mass in g/mol is the same as the molecular mass in u.
5. “Limiting reactant and yield” mode, “Combustion of methane”, with the starting values: 4 mol CH₄ and 10 mol O₂. Ask how much CO₂ will form: 4 mol, that is, 176.04 g. Press “▶ React” and check it in the table.
6. Lower “Yield” to 75 % in “Formation of water” (8 mol H₂ and 5 mol O₂). 6 mol H₂O (108.09 g) form out of a theoretical 8 (144.12 g): “Yield = actual / theoretical · 100 = 75 %”.

### Bachillerato 1: laws of chemical combination, limiting reactant and yield

*Ages 16–17 · Year 12 in the UK · 11th grade in the US*

**Curriculum content.** Block B: “Applying the fundamental laws of chemistry to understand the stoichiometric relationships in chemical reactions and in the composition of compounds”, with “Lavoisier's law of conservation of mass, Proust's law of definite proportions and Dalton's law of multiple proportions. Percentage composition of a compound” and “Stoichiometric calculations in chemical reactions. Purity of a reactant. Yield of a reaction. Limiting reactant and reactant in excess”. Also “Synthesis, substitution, double substitution, decomposition and combustion reactions” and “Observing different types of reaction and checking their stoichiometry”.

**Objectives.** Relate the laws of chemical combination to Dalton's atomic theory. Calculate percentage composition. Identify the limiting reactant and calculate the theoretical product, the excess and the yield.

**Teaching sequence (15–20 min).**

1. Proust mode, “Water (H₂O)”: the percentage composition is 11.2 % H and 88.8 % O, and m(O)/m(H) = 7.94. Do two or three experiments with different amounts and check that the ratio does not change.
2. Switch to “Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂)”: m(O)/m(H) = 15.87, exactly twice 7.94. Repeat with “Carbon monoxide (CO)” (1.33) and “Carbon dioxide (CO₂)” (2.66). State the law of multiple proportions and explain it with atoms: the second compound has twice as many oxygen atoms for each atom of the other element.
3. “Limiting reactant and yield” mode, “Formation of water”, with the starting values (8 H₂ and 5 O₂). Ask for a prediction in “Your prediction: which reactant runs out?”. Many students choose O₂ because there is less of it. React: the limiting reactant is H₂, “In excess: O₂ (only 4 of 5 are needed)”.
4. Solve the same case on the board with “One mole (mass in g)”: 16.13 g of H₂ and 159.99 g of O₂ give 144.12 g of H₂O, with 32.00 g of O₂ left over. The total mass bar shows 176.12 g before and after.
5. Set 8 H₂ and 4 O₂: “Stoichiometric ratio: there is no limiting reactant and none in excess”.
6. Go back to 8 H₂ and 5 O₂ and lower “Yield” to 75 %: 6 H₂O form and 2 H₂ and 2 O₂ are left unreacted. Mention that in industry the yield is lowered by incomplete reactions, side reactions and losses when separating the product.

### Bachillerato 2 Chemistry: occasional review

*Ages 17–18 · Year 13 in the UK · 12th grade in the US*

There is no specific content. It can be used for review before “Stoichiometric calculations and redox titrations” (block B, 5). The simulation only balances by inspection and does not cover the “ion-electron method”; it is worth reminding students that this method is also based on conserving atoms, and charge as well.

## Misconceptions it helps to address

The simulation mainly tackles the idea that balancing is a numbers game with no connection to atoms. Each row gives a common prior idea and how to break it down with the simulation.

| Students' idea | Correct idea | How to show it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “You can change the subscripts to balance an equation” | Changing a subscript changes the substance; only the coefficients are changed | Balancing mode: there are buttons only for the coefficients. Proust mode: H₂O and H₂O₂ have different ratios (7.94 and 15.87) because they are different substances |
| “Mass is lost in a reaction (when something burns, it weighs less)” | The total mass is conserved because the atoms are conserved | “Combustion of methane” balanced: 80.04 u on each side of the balance. In the reactor, the total mass bar does not change while it reacts |
| “If the equation is balanced, there are the same molecules on each side” | Atoms are conserved, not molecules | 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O: 3 molecules against 2, but 6 atoms on each side |
| “The coefficients give the ratio by mass” | They give the ratio in particles or in moles | 2 H₂ + O₂: there are twice as many H₂ molecules, but the O₂ contributes 32.00 u against 4.03 u |
| “The limiting reactant is the one there is least of” | It is the one that runs out first according to the ratio in the equation | Starting values in limiting reactant mode: with 8 H₂ and 5 O₂, the limiting reactant is H₂ |
| “If I add more of a reactant, more product always forms” | If that reactant is in excess, the product does not change | Proust mode, water: with 4 O₂ fixed, going from 8 to 24 H₂ does not change the 8 H₂O; only what is left over increases |
| “With a 100 % yield nothing is left over” | At 100 % the limiting reactant runs out; the reactant in excess is still left over | Starting values in limiting reactant mode: 1 O₂ is left over with a 100 % yield |
| “The equation is better balanced with bigger numbers” | The smallest whole-number coefficients are used | “Formation of water” with 4, 2 and 4: “Balanced, but it can be simplified: divide the coefficients by 2” |

## Simplifications worth knowing

The simulation puts the conservation of atoms and the ratio in the equation first, and to do so it simplifies the shape of the molecules, the mechanism and the amounts. It is worth knowing about these liberties so as not to reinforce wrong ideas by accident.

1. **The molecules are flat diagrams.** The positions of the atoms are approximate and two-dimensional: methane is drawn as a cross rather than a tetrahedron, and the bond angles are not the real ones.
2. **Ionic solids, metals and solid non-metals are separate particles.** NaCl, MgO, Fe₂O₃, Al₂O₃, CaCO₃, KClO₃, FeS, CaO and KCl are drawn as a formula unit, although they are crystal lattices. The metals (Na, Mg, Al, Fe), carbon and sulfur appear as isolated atoms; real sulfur forms S₈ molecules.
3. **The atoms fly straight to the products.** Collisions, step-by-step bond breaking, intermediates and energy are not shown. That is what the rate of reaction simulation is for.
4. **Not every equation happens as written.** Some have been chosen for their teaching value: hydrogen peroxide is not made by combining H₂ and O₂, and NO₂ forms in two steps. Physical states and conditions are not shown either.
5. **The amounts go up in steps of the coefficient.** In Proust and limiting reactant modes, each reactant is chosen in multiples of its coefficient (up to a maximum of 24 particles), so that the limiting reactant always runs out completely. In the laboratory the amounts are continuous.
6. **Yield is modelled by stopping the reaction.** With a yield below 100 %, the reaction stops earlier and part of both reactants is left unreacted. In reality side reactions and losses when separating the product also play a part. Also, the actual product is rounded to whole particles: with few particles the yield obtained may not match the one chosen, and then “(rounded to whole particles)” is shown.
7. **One mole per particle.** With “One mole (mass in g)” each particle drawn represents 6.022·10²³ particles. The amounts are always whole numbers of moles.
8. **Atomic masses with more decimal places than the textbook.** The IUPAC values are used (H = 1.008; O = 15.999…). That is why m(O)/m(H) for water comes out as 7.94 and not 8, as it would with H = 1 and O = 16. It is worth warning students if they check the calculations with rounded masses.
9. **The coefficients go from 1 to 9.** That is enough for every equation on the list, whose largest coefficient is 7 (ethane).
