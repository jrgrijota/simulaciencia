# Teacher guide: changes of state simulation

## Quick reference

The simulation heats or cools a substance and shows the container, its particles and the temperature–time curve at the same time, with a second mode for the phase diagram of water and CO₂. Its main use is in ESO 2 and ESO 3 (Years 9 and 10 in the UK, 8th and 9th grade in the US), with the phase diagram as a resource for ESO 4.

## How the simulation works

The simulation has two modes, “Heat and cool” (heating curve) and “Pressure and temperature” (phase diagram), chosen at the top of the left-hand panel. The first shows three panels: “WHAT WE SEE” (container, hotplate and thermometer), “WHAT WE CANNOT SEE” (64 particles and a legend of states) and “THE GRAPH”. The second shows the diagram, the particle window and the “THINK AND CHECK” panel. In both, a strip at the top explains in one sentence what is happening.

| Mode | What the teacher controls | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heat and cool | Substance (Water, Alcohol (ethanol), Nitrogen, Iron). Hotplate power, from −100 % (cool) to +100 % (heat) in steps of 5; it starts at +50 %. Speed ×1, ×2 or ×4. “Pause” and “Restart” buttons | The container with solid, liquid, bubbles or droplets on the lid. The thermometer with the melting and boiling marks. The temperature–time graph with each plateau labelled. The Temperature, Time, Plate and “State of the substance” cards |
| Heat and cool, particle view | “Show the attractions between particles” and “Show the speed (trails)” checkboxes | The same 64 particles in every state: an 8 × 8 lattice that vibrates, particles that slide around or free particles. The attractions are strong in the solid, weak in the liquid and absent in the gas |
| Pressure and temperature, water | Temperature from −40 to 160 °C. Pressure from 0.003 to 5 atm on a logarithmic scale. Examples “Sea level”, “Top of Everest”, “Pressure cooker” and “Triple point”. The point can also be dragged | The SOLID, LIQUID and GAS regions, the 1 atm line with “melts at 0 °C” and “boils at 100 °C”, and the triple point. The “At this pressure, the substance…” card gives the temperatures of the changes at that pressure |
| Pressure and temperature, CO₂ | Temperature from −120 to 30 °C. Pressure from 0.30 to 100 atm. Examples “Dry ice”, “Open air”, “CO₂ extinguisher” and “Triple point” | The same diagram for CO₂, with its triple point at 5.11 atm and −57 °C. At 1 atm it shows “sublimes at −78 °C” |

Four elements support the explanation:

- **The plateau on the graph.** During melting and boiling the curve goes flat and the label “constant temperature” appears. The thermometer stops at the same time.
- **The energy bar.** The “Where did the energy supplied go?” card has two parts: “Raising the temperature” and “Separating the particles”. It only counts the energy that has gone in since “Restart”, so it starts at 0 %. During a plateau only the second part grows. If more energy has been removed than went in, it changes to “Where did the energy removed come from?”.
- **The double view.** What happens in the container appears in the particles at the same time. The composition bar under the hotplate and the legend give the percentage of solid, liquid and gas.
- **The point on the diagram.** When it crosses a line, the strip names the change (MELTING, VAPORISATION, SUBLIMATION…). It also says whether it was caused by heating or by changing the pressure. On a line, it says which states coexist.

The “Key concepts” drop-down summarises the six changes of state and three ideas: constant temperature during the change, temperatures characteristic of each substance, and particles that do not change. In diagram mode, “How to read a phase diagram” explains regions, lines, the triple point and the compressed scale.

## Use in each year group

In ESO 2 the simulation is used to introduce the states of matter and changes of state with the kinetic theory; in ESO 3, to go deeper into the kinetic-molecular model and the energy involved in the changes; in ESO 4, to relate changes of state to pressure. The curriculum content quoted is from the LOMLOE Physics and Chemistry curriculum of the Community of Madrid (Spain), in our own translation.

### ESO 2: states and changes of state

*Ages 13–14 · Year 9 in the UK · 8th grade in the US*

**Curriculum content.** Block A: “Graphical representation of results” and “the laboratory and virtual environments”. Block B: “Introduction to the kinetic-molecular theory. States of matter”. Block E: “Temperature. Temperature scales. The concept of heat” and “Effects of heat on matter: changes of state and expansion”.

**Objectives.** Describe solid, liquid and gas with the particle model. Name the six changes of state. Recognise that the temperature does not change during melting and boiling. Associate each substance with its melting and boiling points.

**Teaching sequence (15–20 min).**

1. Heat and cool mode with water (starting set-up). Set the hotplate to 0 %: ice at about −20 °C. Show the magnifier: ordered particles vibrating in place. Read the “SOLID” legend.
2. Choose speed ×2, raise the hotplate to +50 % and press “Restart”: the ice goes back to −20 °C and the time to 0. Ask what the thermometer will read while the ice melts. At 5 min on the Time card it reaches 0 °C.
3. Point out the plateau: the temperature stays at 0 °C until 33 min. In the magnifier the particles leave the lattice; in the container the ice floats and gets smaller.
4. The liquid warms up to 100 °C at 85 min; on the graph it rises more slowly than the ice did. Ask what the bubbles are. The magnifier shows they are the same particles, now separated. The plateau lasts until 137 min.
5. On reaching 140 °C, “LIMIT” appears. Set the hotplate to −50 %: droplets on the lid (condensation) and then freezing. Open “Key concepts” and name the six changes.
6. Switch to Alcohol (melts at −114 °C, boils at 78 °C) and to Nitrogen (−210 °C and −196 °C). The curve has the same stages and its plateaus last the same time; what changes is the temperatures. Conclude that these are characteristic properties.

### ESO 3: kinetic-molecular model and the energy of the changes

*Ages 14–15 · Year 10 in the UK · 9th grade in the US*

**Curriculum content.** Block A: “Recording data and results using tables, graphs and mathematical expressions”. Block B: “Further study of the kinetic-molecular model of matter and its relationship with changes of state” and “Kinetic-molecular model of matter. Changes of state of matter”.

**Objectives.** Use the particle model to explain why the temperature does not change on a plateau. Distinguish the energy that raises the temperature from the energy that separates particles. Interpret a heating curve and a cooling curve. Recognise sublimation.

**Teaching sequence (15–20 min).**

1. Water, hotplate at +100 %, speed ×2 and “Restart”. Turn on both particle-view checkboxes. Relate the vibration of the solid and the speed of the liquid to the temperature.
2. When melting has finished, read the energy bar: 16 % “Raising the temperature” and 84 % “Separating the particles”. When boiling has finished it shows 41 % and 59 %. Vaporisation needs more energy than melting: the 100 °C plateau is almost twice as long as the 0 °C one.
3. Set the hotplate to +50 %, press “Restart” and repeat. Melting takes 28 min instead of 14, but it still happens at 0 °C. More power shortens the plateau; it does not raise the temperature of the change.
4. Halfway through melting, set the hotplate to 0 %. The state changes to “Solid and liquid together” and the strip to EQUILIBRIUM. With no exchange of energy, the proportions do not change.
5. Heat it up to gas at 140 °C, set the hotplate to −100 % and discuss the cooling curve: the same plateaus, now CONDENSATION and FREEZING. Switch to Nitrogen and read its temperatures off the graph to identify it.
6. Go to Pressure and temperature, CO₂, “Dry ice” example (1 atm, −100 °C). Raise the temperature: at around −78 °C the solid turns straight into gas. The strip says SUBLIMATION.

### ESO 4: changes of state and pressure

*Ages 15–16 · Year 11 in the UK · 10th grade in the US*

**Curriculum content.** Block D: “The concept of pressure. Hydrostatic pressure. Atmospheric pressure” and “Physics of the atmosphere”. Block E: “Energy transfers: work and heat as ways of transferring energy between systems, related to forces or temperature differences” and “Effect of heat on objects”.

**Objectives.** Read a phase diagram. Explain that the boiling point depends on pressure. Justify the pressure cooker and cooking at high altitude. Locate the triple point and the sublimation of dry ice.

**Teaching sequence (15–20 min).**

1. Pressure and temperature mode, water, “Sea level” example (1 atm, 20 °C): state Liquid. Point out the 1 atm line, “melts at 0 °C” and “boils at 100 °C”. Warn that the pressure scale is compressed.
2. Collapse “Reading the diagram”, which would give the answer away. “Top of Everest” example (0.33 atm). Ask at what temperature it will boil. Raise the temperature with the slider: between 70 and 73 °C the state is “Liquid + gas”. The strip confirms that it boils at 72 °C.
3. “Pressure cooker” example (2 atm). The “At this pressure, the substance…” card says it boils at 121 °C. Relate higher pressure to a higher cooking temperature.
4. Go back to “Sea level” and lower only the pressure. At around 0.023 atm water boils at 20 °C. The strip says “Lowering the pressure makes the liquid boil without heating it!”.
5. “Triple point” example (0.006 atm and 0 °C): all three states coexist. Below that pressure there is no liquid; at 0.005 atm ice sublimes at −2 °C.
6. Switch to CO₂. “Open air” (1 atm, 20 °C) is a gas. “CO₂ extinguisher” (65 atm) is a liquid and will boil at 26 °C. At 1 atm CO₂ is never a liquid.

### Bachillerato 1: occasional review

*Ages 16–17 · Year 12 in the UK · 11th grade in the US*

The simulation fits as review when dealing with “Heat as a mechanism for transferring energy between two bodies” and “Internal energy of a system. First law of thermodynamics” (block F). During a plateau heat goes in and the temperature does not change: the energy bar lets you discuss that the internal energy increases. The attractions checkbox serves as a bridge to “Intermolecular forces” (block A), comparing the 100 °C of water with the −196 °C of nitrogen.

### Bachillerato 2 Chemistry: occasional review

*Ages 17–18 · Year 13 in the UK · 12th grade in the US*

On the diagram, on any line or at the triple point, the particles swap state every 0.65 s and are marked with a ring. It illustrates “Chemical equilibrium as a dynamic process” (block B) without needing a reaction. The boiling points of water, ethanol and nitrogen serve as an introduction to “Macroscopic properties of elements and molecular compounds” (block A).

## Misconceptions it helps to address

The simulation mainly tackles the idea that particles change when the state changes, and the confusion between heat and temperature. Each row gives a common prior idea and how to break it down with the simulation.

| Students' idea | Correct idea | How to show it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “If I keep heating, boiling water keeps getting hotter” | During boiling the temperature stays constant | Water at +50 %: the thermometer stays at 100 °C from 85 to 137 min and the graph is labelled “constant temperature” |
| “With a bigger flame water boils at a higher temperature” | More power shortens the plateau but does not change the boiling point | Repeat at +100 %: boiling takes 26 min instead of 52 and still happens at 100 °C |
| “The bubbles in boiling water are air” | They are the same substance in the gas state | Watch the magnifier during BOILING: the particles of the liquid escape as gas. The container is closed and there are no other particles |
| “When they melt or evaporate, the particles expand, soften or change” | The particles are the same; their order, spacing and movement change | Go along the water curve: there are always 64 particles of the same size and colour. Read “Key concepts” |
| “In a solid the particles are still” | The particles in a solid vibrate about their position, more so the hotter it is | With the hotplate at 0 %, compare the ice at −40 °C and at −5 °C in the magnifier (with “Pause” the particles freeze) |
| “There is air between the particles of a gas” | There is only empty space between the particles | Water vapour at 120 °C: the particle window is empty between them |
| “Cooling means adding cold” | Cooling means removing energy | Hotplate at −50 %: the label says “COOLING 50 % · energy out” and the arrows point out of the container |
| “Water always boils at 100 °C” | The boiling point depends on the pressure | Water diagram: “Top of Everest” boils at 72 °C and “Pressure cooker” at 121 °C |

## Simplifications worth knowing

The simulation makes sure the plateaus and the particle model are clearly visible, and to do so it scales energies and times. It is worth knowing about these liberties so as not to reinforce wrong ideas by accident.

1. **The same energies for every substance.** Melting absorbs 14 units and vaporisation 26 in all four substances. The three heating stages share 36 units according to how much the temperature rises in each. The temperatures are real; in reality each substance has its own specific and latent heats.
2. **The proportions between stages are not real.** Vaporisation absorbs 26 units against 14 for melting, about 1.9 times more. In real water it is about 6.8 times more (2257 against 334 J/g). It is enough to say “more energy”.
3. **The liquid always needs twice as much per degree.** In all four substances the liquid needs twice as much energy per degree as the solid and the gas, so its section of the curve is less steep. That is the case for water (4.18 against about 2 J/g·°C), but for alcohol, nitrogen or iron the real ratio is different. Gradients can be compared within water, not between substances.
4. **Time is not real.** Each “min” on the Time card is one real second at ×1. With water at +50 %, the whole curve takes 147 min. The time figures are only for comparing stages with each other.
5. **A movable lid keeps 1 atm.** The container is closed with a lid that moves freely, so the pressure does not change and the plateaus are the 1 atm ones. In a rigid container the pressure would rise as vapour formed and there would be no plateau at 100 °C; the hint and the container label say so. Also, no gas appears until the boiling point: evaporation below it is not represented. It is worth adding this nuance when talking about washing drying on the line.
6. **Agitation does not depend on absolute temperature.** Each state spreads its agitation over its own temperature range. The vibration of the solid goes from 0.8 to 3.2 px and the speed of the gas from 3.2 to 4.6. Nitrogen gas at −188 °C moves just like water vapour at 120 °C. To compare substances, use the thermometer, not the magnifier.
7. **In the magnifier the solid is less compact than the liquid.** The lattice has its particles 21 px apart and the liquid brings them to 18 px. That fits ice, which floats, but not alcohol, nitrogen or iron. In the container, on the other hand, only ice floats.
8. **The phase diagram is schematic.** Pressure is on a logarithmic scale. The melting line of water has the real gradient (−0.0075 °C/atm), but at 5 atm it only drops to −0.03 °C and looks vertical. It cannot be used to show that anomaly. CO₂ has no critical point: its curve ends at 30 °C and 71.5 atm.
9. **The lines have thickness.** The point is on a line if it is less than 5 px away, and on the triple point if less than 12 px. That is why “Liquid + gas” appears over an interval, from 70 to 73 °C on Everest. On the line the particles are split half and half, whereas in reality the proportion depends on the energy supplied.
