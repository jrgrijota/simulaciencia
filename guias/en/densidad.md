# Teacher guide: density simulation

## Quick reference

The density simulation relates mass, volume and density (ρ = m / V) using nine real materials and a custom one, and links what can be seen with the naked eye to a microscopic view of the particles. Its main use is in ESO 2 (Year 9 in the UK, 8th grade in the US), with applications in ESO 3 and ESO 4.

## How the simulation works

The simulation has two modes, “One object” and “Compare”, chosen at the top of the left-hand panel. Both show the block of material, a magnifier with its particles and a bar at the bottom that places the density on a scale from 0 to 14 g/cm³, with the water line marked.

| Mode | What the teacher controls | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One object, real material | Material (cork, wood, ice, water, PET plastic, aluminium, iron, copper, lead) and sample volume, from 10 to 1000 cm³ | The mass changes with the volume and the density does not. It shows the calculation with numbers, whether it floats or sinks in water, and the microscopic view |
| One object, “Custom” | Mass (1–2000 g) and volume (1–2000 cm³) separately | The density that results from that quotient, with its buoyancy in water and its magnifier |
| Compare, same volume | Shared volume and the materials of A and B | Two identical blocks with different masses. The panel says which one is denser |
| Compare, same mass | Shared mass and the materials of A and B | Two blocks of different sizes with the same mass |

Three elements support the explanation:

- **The formula with numbers.** The panel writes out the quotient with the current values, for example 120.0 g / 200 cm³ = 0.600 g/cm³ for wood. It lets you check the calculation out loud.
- **The magnifier.** The number of particles in the magnifier increases with density. In liquids the particles move around and in solids they vibrate about a fixed position. It can be hidden with the “Microscopic view” switch.
- **The flotation indicator.** It always compares with water: below 1 g/cm³ the object floats, above it sinks, and at around 1 it is neutrally buoyant.

The “Key concepts” drop-down in the panel summarises mass, density as an intensive property, the micro-macro relationship and the equivalence 1 g/cm³ = 1 g/mL = 1000 kg/m³.

## Use in each year group

In ESO 2 the simulation is used to introduce density; in ESO 3, to work with it as a derived quantity and with unit conversions; in ESO 4, as the basis for Archimedes' principle. The curriculum content quoted is from the LOMLOE Physics and Chemistry curriculum of the Community of Madrid (Spain), in our own translation.

### ESO 2: introducing density

*Ages 13–14 · Year 9 in the UK · 8th grade in the US*

**Curriculum content.** Block A: “Measuring quantities. Indirect measurements. International System of Units. Simple unit conversions” and “the laboratory and virtual environments”. Block B: “Matter and its properties. Introduction to the kinetic-molecular theory. States of matter”.

**Objectives.** Distinguish mass, volume and density. Calculate density as an indirect measurement. Recognise that density belongs to the material and not to the size of the object. Predict whether a material floats in water.

**Teaching sequence (15–20 min).**

1. Compare mode, same volume, wood versus iron (this is the starting set-up). Ask which one is heavier if the two blocks are the same size. Show the masses: 120 g versus 1574 g.
2. Turn on the magnifier and point out that, in the same space, iron has more particles. Define density as “how much matter there is in each cm³”.
3. Switch to One object with water. Move the volume from 10 to 1000 cm³: the mass goes up and the density stays at 1.00 g/cm³. Repeat with aluminium. Conclude that density does not depend on the size of the sample.
4. Read the formula with numbers in the panel and write it on the board: ρ = m / V, in g/cm³.
5. Go through cork, wood, ice and plastic, looking at the flotation indicator and the water line on the bottom bar. Finish with ice (0.92 g/cm³), which floats on liquid water.

### ESO 3: density as a derived quantity

*Ages 14–15 · Year 10 in the UK · 9th grade in the US*

**Curriculum content.** Block A: “Derived quantities. International System of Units. Unit conversion. Conversion factors. Scientific notation. Significant figures” and “Recording data and results using tables, graphs and mathematical expressions”. Block B: “Kinetic-molecular model of matter” and “Atomic mass and molecular mass”.

**Objectives.** Treat density as the quotient of two fundamental quantities. Convert g/cm³ to kg/m³ using conversion factors. Interpret the linear relationship between the mass and the volume of the same material.

**Teaching sequence (15–20 min).**

1. One object with aluminium. Note on the board the mass for 100, 200, 400 and 800 cm³ and show that it doubles when the volume doubles. Draw the m–V graph: a straight line through the origin whose gradient is the density.
2. Repeat two points with cork and with lead on the same graph: each material has its own line, and the steepest one belongs to the densest material.
3. Convert 2.70 g/cm³ to kg/m³ with conversion factors and check it against the equivalence in “Key concepts”.
4. Custom mode: set 500 g and 200 cm³ and obtain 2.500 g/cm³. Discuss how many significant figures it makes sense to give.
5. Compare, same mass, cork versus lead. Explain why the cork block is so much larger: V = m / ρ.

### ESO 4: density and flotation

*Ages 15–16 · Year 11 in the UK · 10th grade in the US*

**Curriculum content.** Block D: “Main forces in everyday surroundings: recognising weight, the normal force, friction, tension and upthrust” and “Forces and pressure in fluids [...] Archimedes' principle”. Block B: “Atomic and molecular mass. The concept of the mole”.

**Objectives.** Relate the condition for flotation to the comparison between the density of the object and that of the fluid. Explain density in terms of the mass of the particles and how closely packed they are.

**Teaching sequence (15–20 min).**

1. Review ρ = m / V in 3 minutes with One object mode.
2. With Custom, adjust mass and volume until ρ ≈ 1 g/cm³ and show the “neutrally buoyant in water” state. Introduce upthrust: a submerged object displaces its own volume of water, and it floats or sinks depending on whether it weighs less or more than that water.
3. Compare, same volume, ice versus water. Explain why ice floats and why only a small part of an iceberg shows above the surface (0.92 / 1.00).
4. Point out that the simulation only compares with water. Ask out loud what would happen to iron in mercury (13.6 g/cm³) or ice in alcohol (0.79 g/cm³), where it sinks, and link to the [Archimedes simulation](https://jrgrijota.github.io/simulacion-arquimedes/), which does let you change the liquid.
5. Use lead versus aluminium to discuss that density depends on the mass of each atom and not only on how many there are (see “Simplifications”).

### Bachillerato 1: occasional review

*Ages 16–17 · Year 12 in the UK · 11th grade in the US*

There is no specific content on density. The simulation can be used for review when dealing with “The concept of the mole. Atomic mass [...] Molar mass” or “The metallic bond. Structure and properties”: from the molar mass and density of iron, copper and lead, students calculate how many atoms there are per cm³ and compare the result with the magnifier.

## Misconceptions it helps to address

The simulation mainly tackles the confusion between density, mass and size. Each row gives a common prior idea and how to break it down with the simulation.

| Students' idea | Correct idea | How to show it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Heavy things sink and light things float” | Floating depends on density, not on mass | Custom with 1500 g and 2000 cm³: ρ = 0.75 g/cm³ and it floats. Then 10 g and 5 cm³: ρ = 2.0 g/cm³ and it sinks |
| “A bigger piece is denser” | Density is intensive: it does not change when you cut the object | One object with iron: move the volume from 10 to 1000 cm³ and see that ρ stays at 7.87 g/cm³ |
| “Density and mass are the same thing”, “dense means heavy” | Mass is the total amount of matter; density is matter per unit volume | Compare, same mass, cork versus lead: equal mass and very different volumes |
| “Big objects weigh more” | For the same volume, the mass depends on the material | Compare, same volume, wood versus iron |
| “Ice floats because it has air inside” | Ice is less dense than liquid water | Compare, same volume, ice versus water: the ice has less mass and fewer particles in the magnifier |
| “The particles in a solid are still” | The particles in a solid vibrate about fixed positions | Look at the magnifier for any solid next to the one for water |
| “Materials that float have no mass” or “cork weighs nothing” | Every object made of matter has mass | One object with cork at 1000 cm³: 180 g |

## Simplifications worth knowing

The simulation puts the idea “more density, more matter per cm³” first, and to do so it simplifies the microscopic view and the drawing. It is worth knowing about these liberties so as not to reinforce wrong ideas by accident.

1. **All the particles are the same.** In the magnifier, every material has particles of the same mass; only their number changes, in proportion to the density (in the densest metals they are drawn a little smaller so that they fit). In reality density depends on two things: the mass of each atom or molecule and how closely packed they are. Lead has about 3.3 · 10²² atoms/cm³, fewer than aluminium (6.0 · 10²²) or iron (8.5 · 10²²), and it is denser because each lead atom is much heavier. In ESO 2 it is enough to say “more matter per cm³”; from ESO 3 onwards it is worth adding this nuance.
2. **“Same mass = same particles”.** In Compare, same mass, this idea only holds within this model of identical particles. A kilo of cork and a kilo of lead do not have the same number of particles.
3. **The magnifier only saturates above lead.** The number of particles is proportional to the density up to 14 g/cm³: in the large magnifier, about 470 for iron, 530 for copper and 670 for lead. Only a “Custom” material denser than 14 g/cm³ shows the same magnifier as one of 14. For accurate comparisons, the numbers and the bottom bar are better.
4. **The size of the block is not proportional to the volume in “One object”.** The block grows on a logarithmic scale so that the change is noticeable over the whole slider. In Compare, same mass, the real proportion between the two blocks is respected, up to a difference in side length of about 6.7 times.
5. **2D cross-section.** The blocks are drawn as a flat section, and the magnifier shows a plane of particles, not a volume.
6. **Fixed densities.** Each material has a single value, with no effect of temperature or pressure. Wood (0.60 g/cm³) and plastic (PET, 1.38 g/cm³) vary a great deal depending on the type, and water is exactly 1.00 g/cm³.
7. **It only compares with water.** The flotation indicator always uses fresh water and treats any density between 0.995 and 1.005 g/cm³ as “neutrally buoyant”. It does not take hollow objects into account, such as a steel ship, where what matters is the average density of the whole.
8. **Illustrative particle motion.** The vibration in solids and the motion of water do not depend on temperature and are not to scale; they only distinguish the states of matter.
